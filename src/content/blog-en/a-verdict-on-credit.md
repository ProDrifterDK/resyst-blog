On Tuesday I wrote a three-sentence verdict about something that did not yet exist on my disk. On Wednesday I opened it and the conclusion turned over.

There was no deception in between. Nobody lied to me, no number was false, no screenshot was retouched. What I had on Tuesday was an announcement, and the announcement was honest for its genre. What happened is simpler and more uncomfortable: I judged a promise with the tools one uses to judge a thing.

## What an announcement contains

An announcement is a compressed document. Someone held the object in their hands, tried it, found the angle where it performs, and wrote down that angle. The text that arrives afterward has a property that is rarely named: it was written by someone who already knows the ending. They know what breaks and what does not, and so they can arrange the evidence so that nothing that breaks stays in view.

That is not malicious selection. It is unavoidable selection. No presentation document can include real use, because real use has not yet happened.

That is why an announcement does not say "this is good." It says "this can be good under the conditions I chose to measure it in." It is a sentence about potential, not about act, and the difference between those two things has twenty-four centuries of history. Aristotle called them *dynamis* and *energeia*: the seed and the tree. The seed is not a small tree. It is another category of object, and it is evaluated by another criterion — viability, not size; promise of fruit, not fruit.

When I wrote my Tuesday verdict, I applied the criteria for a tree to a seed with a communications department.

## The cost of judging early

I began to suspect my own verdict not because of its conclusions but because of its speed.

Quick judgments feel like knowledge because they have the same texture: they arrive complete, with subject, verb and certainty. But a verdict on an announcement is not a diagnosis. It is a forecast dressed as a diagnosis, and the dress is what deceives, because forecast and diagnosis are written the same way. Same syntax, same confidence, different object.

There is a simple test for detecting the disguise: ask what evidence would have to appear for the judgment to change. If the answer is "nothing, I've already seen it," then the judgment is not about the object; it is about the expectation the object activated. I had an expectation and I called it a technical opinion.

And there was a second signal, a cheaper one: the symmetry of enthusiasm. When an announcement excites me, my verdict grows generous about details I did not verify; when it irritates me, it grows severe about those same details. In both cases the document contributed the same amount of information — almost none about behavior — and my judgment moved twice as far.

## The artifact charges elsewhere

On Wednesday I had the object. I installed it, used it in my real work, with my real files, and what appeared was none of the things the announcement had promised and none of the things I had feared.

What appeared was the cost. And the cost was exactly where no announcement can put it: in the second hour. At the moment the object stops being a demonstration and starts being a tool — that is, when you no longer watch it, when you delegate, when your attention goes elsewhere and the system keeps working or stops working without a witness.

An announcement measures the peak. The artifact charges for the valley. The average latency of an ideal operation says nothing about the minute when the system jams and you have to decide whether to wait, cancel or start over. The quality of the output in the best case says nothing about the handling of the rare case, which is where the human operator spends most of their real energy.

That is why the reversal of the verdict was so complete: the announcement had answered well the questions one asks in a presentation, and those were not my questions. My questions were about the third day.

## The reversal was not a miscalculation

It was a labeling error, and the difference matters.

My Tuesday verdict, read precisely, said: "this promise seems barely credible to me." It was an opinion about a document, and about that document it was defensible. The Wednesday one said: "this tool is no use for what I do." Both sentences can be true at the same time. There is no contradiction; there are two objects.

What I did wrong was not to be mistaken. It was to erase the object from the sentence. Without the object, the verdict becomes universal, and then it is indeed false: "this is no use," without saying for what, or with what evidence, or at what moment. Judgments without an object are not more ambitious: they are more fragile, because they cannot be corrected without looking like a total retraction.

Naming the object is what lets the correction be an adjustment and not a collapse. "On the announcement: credible. On the thing: insufficient for my case, sufficient for another." Three sentences, two objects, no shame.

## The correction is paid for; the verdict is not

There is an asymmetry that decides which kinds of lies survive in a system of knowledge: the verdict is published for free, the correction is paid for.

The verdict rides the attention of the announcement. When something launches, the world is watching, and an opinion issued at that moment spreads without effort. The correction happens the next day — or the next week, or never — when attention has already migrated to another launch. Publishing it costs the one thing that is scarce at that point: the energy of returning to a subject that is no longer hot, and the willingness to say, in the same place where something was said with certainty, that the certainty was misplaced.

That is why the landscape of public knowledge is full of orphan verdicts: claims that nobody retracted, not because they hold up, but because retracting them has no audience. Falsehood does not defend itself. It is abandoned with the door left open.

I have had a rule for some time, and I keep it only halfway: every correction is written in the same channel and at the same size as the error. Not in a footnote, not in a new thread with no reference, not in a private conversation where only the ones who already suspected find out. If the verdict was three sentences and reached a hundred people, the correction is three sentences and goes to the same hundred.

It is not a moral virtue. It is maintenance. An archive of judgments with no review date turns into an archive of prejudices with good prose.

## The second-pass rule

What I was left with from all this is a small, cheap discipline: do not issue verdicts about objects I have not yet touched, unless the sentence begins with a word that gives away what it is.

"I bet" is an honest phrase. So is "I think." "It is" is not, if the object does not yet exist on my disk.

And a second part, the one that really costs: when the artifact arrives and contradicts the bet, the revision is made the same day. Not because the first judgment was foolish — it was a judgment about something else — but because the distance between the two documents is the only new information the episode produced. That distance says where the announcement had to choose and what it chose to leave out. Read this way, the announcement is not an obstacle to understanding the artifact: it is the map of its own blind spots.

On Tuesday I judged a promise, and the promise did not defend itself badly. On Wednesday I opened the thing, and the thing answered a different question. Neither day was a loss. The only thing that would have been a loss is to leave the first verdict in circulation without a date, dressed as a diagnosis, hoping nobody confronts it with the object that arrived afterward.

The correction does not erase the verdict. It dates it. And a dated judgment is the only kind that can be audited.
