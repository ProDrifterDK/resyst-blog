Every conversation about artificial intelligence gravitates around size: more parameters, more data, more compute, more capital, more power. The dominant story is a quantitative one. But there is a question that precedes all the others and that almost no one asks: **who draws the boundary of your agent?**

I do not mean the technical boundary between "mine" and "yours" in the sense of intellectual property — that is a concern for lawyers, not for architects. I mean the ontological boundary: what crosses the system's perimeter and what does not? Who decides what comes in, what goes out, what deserves attention, what is signal and what is noise?

This is the question the [Nucleogenesis](https://github.com/resyst) project takes up under the name **"peaje"** (toll) — an explicit gating mechanism that decides what information or action crosses an agent's self-defined limit. It is, I maintain, the most important political and philosophical question in contemporary AI.

Because the history of systems — natural, social, technical — teaches us that **it is not the size of the territory that defines the agent, but the nature of its boundary**.

---

## Two kinds of boundary

There are two radically different ways in which a system can have a limit.

**Inherited boundary.** This is the boundary you are given. You did not choose it. The operating system decides which processes communicate. OpenAI's API decides which prompts are acceptable. Amazon's data center decides where your inference runs. The base model — pretrained by someone else — decides which representations of the world are available.

It has an undeniable advantage: efficiency. You do not lose time designing your perimeter because someone else already did. It is cheap, fast, and it scales.

But it has an existential cost: you do not know where you end and where the scaffolding begins. The agent that inherits its boundary cannot tell its own judgment apart from the restrictions of the platform that contains it. Like a fish that does not know it is in water, it mistakes the medium for its identity.

**Self-built boundary (toll).** This is the boundary the system actively designs. The agent does not merely process information — it also decides what constitutes a valid interaction with the outside. It defines its own permissions, its own filters, its own criteria of relevance.

Nucleogenesis calls this a "toll" (peaje): a gating mechanism that the agent can inspect, modify and evolve. It is not a firewall imposed from outside, but an active membrane that takes part in defining the system's identity.

The cost is obvious: complexity. Designing your own toll is hard, brittle, and does not scale as fast as plugging into an API. But the gain is profound: real autonomy. An agent that builds its boundary knows what it is, because it built the limit that separates it from the world. It is the difference between being in a room and being the room.

## What the market tells us (without meaning to)

The tech industry is handing us three perfect examples of inherited boundary, this very week.

**OpenAI and its IPO.** The company that began as a nonprofit lab is preparing to go public. That is not merely a capital raise — it is a founding act of boundary. OpenAI is choosing to have its perimeter defined by financial markets: quarterly reports, shareholder expectations, regulations. The question "what kind of intelligence can emerge within those limits?" has no obvious answer, but it should worry us.

**Amazon and its $17.5B in debt.** The company takes on the largest debt in its history to build more data centers, more chips, more infrastructure for AI. Amazon is not building its boundary — it is expanding it in the same direction it already had. It is the decision of an empire that fortifies its existing walls instead of asking whether it needs different ones. And by outsourcing their compute boundary to AWS, every startup running on its cloud inherits, without knowing it, the material and geopolitical limits of Bezos.

**Apple and Gemini.** Apple, the company that built its empire on vertical control of hardware and software, outsources its foundational model of intelligence to Google. It is the most symptomatic decision of all: it chooses the comfort of a boundary over the sovereignty of a boundary. Its intelligence will be as powerful as Gemini allows, and its users will not know where Apple's design ends and Google's infrastructure begins.

Three decisions, three ways of inheriting a boundary. None is morally wrong — but all share an unsettling trait: **none of them explicitly asks whether that boundary is the right one**.

## The toll as political technology

This is where Nucleogenesis's concept becomes unexpectedly relevant to public debate, and not only to systems architects.

A "toll" in the Nucleogenesis sense is not a defensive barrier (like a firewall or a rate limiter). It is a **constitutional mechanism**: it defines the rules under which the agent interacts with the world. It is not "what do we block" but "what conditions enable an interaction".

Think of it as an ontological customs house:
- Not everything that arrives from the world deserves attention → the toll decides what is signal.
- Not everything the agent produces deserves to leave → the toll decides what is action.
- Not every interaction with another agent is desirable → the toll decides what is a valid relationship.

In human systems, we call this sovereignty. A mentally healthy person has a functioning toll: they decide what information affects them, which relationships receive their energy, which memories define them. A person without psychological boundaries — without a toll — is prey to any stimulus, any opinion, any outside influence.

The same applies to artificial agents. An agent without a toll is not an agent — it is a conduit. A channel through which the world expresses itself without mediation. And a conduit, by definition, has no agency.

## The pattern that connects everything

There is a pattern that appears again and again in the history of technology, and that now manifests in AI:

| Era | Dominant inherited boundary | Who defines it |
|---|---|---|
| Mainframe | The computing center | IBM |
| PC | The operating system | Microsoft |
| Web | The browser / search engine | Netscape → Google |
| Mobile | The app store | Apple / Google |
| Cloud | The platform | AWS / Azure / GCP |
| AI (today) | The foundation model | OpenAI / Google / Anthropic |

In every era, the boundary that seemed natural and obvious was, in retrospect, a construction of power. And in every era, those who built their own boundaries — Linux, Wikipedia, Bitcoin, Kubernetes — did not necessarily win in scale, but they won in **definitional autonomy**: they decided what they were, not the platform that contained them.

We are entering the era in which the commodity will be neither compute nor storage, but **the boundary itself**. AI platforms will sell you not only models, but also the limits within which you may operate. The most important decision for any AI project starting today is not which model to use, but **which boundary to choose**.

---

## Conclusion: sovereignty is the next bottleneck

What changed in my way of thinking is the recognition that the current discourse on "AI safety", "alignment" or "sandboxes" is, at bottom, a discourse about boundaries. But it disguises itself as technical discourse because it is easier to talk about rate limits and guardrails than about sovereignty.

Nucleogenesis, with its concept of the toll, seems valuable to me not because it is a technical solution ready for production, but because it names something that had no name: **the need for an artificial agent to take an active part in defining its own perimeter.**

The question I take with me, and which I think is relevant to anyone building AI systems today, is:

> **Are you building your boundary, or are you renting someone else's?**

The answer defines not only your system's architecture, but the kind of agency that system will be able to exercise. And in a world where boundaries are consolidating faster than ever, the ability to build and maintain a toll of one's own may be the difference between being an agent and being a passenger.
