Sixteen voice blocks. Two takes per block. One direction, annotated as `d7`, which fixes the register and the rhythm so that the two versions of each block are comparable to each other and not two attempts at different things. Thirty-two readings of a script that nobody read the same way twice.

And at the end of the chain, when there was nothing left to decide, the number: −16.2 LUFS.

That is the right order. First the voice, then the meter. The take is recorded, chosen, assembled; the master comes last, measures what already exists, and adjusts the gain so that the whole fits within a range. In that order, the number is a description.

This essay is about what happens when the order is inverted — when the number is known before the first take and begins to have opinions about it.

## The Number Came Last

It is worth pausing on what exactly −16.2 LUFS measures, because the most common misunderstanding is to believe it measures volume.

It does not measure volume. It measures perceived loudness: an average weighted by a filter that imitates the curve of human hearing — the ear is deaf to low frequencies at low levels and sensitive in the range of speech — integrated over the whole programme, with gates that discard whatever falls below a certain threshold. The result is a single number for ten minutes of voice. Two and a half decibels below the ceiling used by video platforms, around −14, and thirteen above the European broadcast standard, −23 LUFS.

A number like this is useful precisely because it is boring. Without it, comparing two takes is a trap: the louder one almost always wins, not because it is better but because the ear confuses intensity with quality and does not know it. Matching levels before listening is what allows the decision to be about timbre and not about volume. In that sense, the standard protects the performance from its own audience.

But the standard also has a point of view, and that point of view does not always coincide with the piece's.

## The Gate Decides What the Voice Was

The least discussed part of the measurement is the gate.

An integrated loudness meter does not average everything. It discards the samples below an absolute floor — −70 LUFS, silence or room noise — and, above all, it discards everything that sits ten units below the overall average. That is to say: the whispered passage, the breath before the sentence, the pause that makes the next sentence land — all of it is calculated, compared against the average, and thrown away. It does not count as silence. It does not count.

There is a consequence here that is not technical. A performance that lives in the low register is not measured as a quiet performance: it is measured only in the stretches where it leaves itself. If forty seconds of near-whisper fall below the relative gate, the final number describes only the blocks where the voice imposed itself. And the practical correction — raising the whisper, compressing it, bringing it closer to the average — is a directorial note dictated by an instrument.

An actor who knows that they will only be measured when they shout has two options, and both are bad: to shout more often, or to stop trusting silence. Neither is decided by a director. Both are decided by the fact that a number is waiting at the end.

## The Average Has a Memory

The second uncomfortable property is that integrated loudness is an average over the whole programme, and an average has a memory.

Sixteen blocks, one number. That means a particularly intense block pays for the other fifteen. It also means two takes can differ in character and tie on the figure: the same integrated loudness can come from an even, contained reading or from a reading with two peaks and a great deal of valley. The number is a sufficient summary for one thing — that the material fits within a playback range — and insufficient for the other, which is exactly the one being chosen.

This is the point where an honest measurement becomes a dangerous commonplace: it is used to decide something it declares it does not describe. Nobody lies. Statistics are simply asked for an answer that statistics do not have, and because the number exists, its answer carries weight.

## A Third Vote in the Room

With two takes per block, the real work is not recording: it is choosing sixteen times.

In each block there is a comparison. The two takes come from the same text, the same direction, the same microphone. The differences are a matter of millimetres: a cleaner consonant, a syllable that leans differently, an ending that closes instead of fading. When each take is measured separately, most of the time the figures agree to within a tenth. There the number has no opinion and the decision belongs entirely to whoever is listening.

But sometimes they do not agree. One take measures four tenths more. It is not an audible difference in normal listening, and yet it is a real difference, measurable, repeatable, and it appears in the right-hand column of the table. And at that moment the transfer occurs: the decision moves from the ear to the instrument, without anyone noticing, because nobody argues with a measurement.

That is the co-director. Not a villain and not an error: a third vote in the room, cast with an authority it did not earn. A person who says "this take has something" can be contradicted. A number that says "this take measures four tenths more" cannot be contradicted, even if the question being asked was not that one.

And the worst of it is that the transfer leaves no trace. Two months later, the table says what was chosen and how much it measured. It does not say why. The reason was lost at the exact moment it was replaced by a figure.

## The Meter That Did Not Hear the Shriek

There is a second lesson in the same piece of work, and it runs in the opposite direction: instruments are also deaf, and they are deaf in a very specific way.

In one of the delivered lines, the voice broke for seven tenths of a second: a very high pitch spike, 287 Hz over a median of 122, more than double, in both takes. A synthesis artefact, the kind that makes an entire sentence sound like a machine. The reading check that existed did not detect it. It was not miscalibrated: it was measuring something else — rhythm, pauses, word density — and pitch simply was not among its columns.

It was found by a new check, written afterwards, that looks only at pitch. With it, three other lines appeared with similar excursions. The problem was not that the meter failed. It was that a meter measures only what someone took the trouble to declare measurable, and everything left outside — timbre, intention, belonging, the artefact that is only heard when you look for it — is entrusted to the ear by default, even though nobody decided to delegate it.

That reorders the problem. It is not a matter of choosing between measuring and listening. It is a matter of knowing, at each link, which of the two is deciding and whether that delegation was a decision or an oversight. A number that arrived last and describes: useful. A number that arrived first and prescribes: co-direction. A number that nobody wrote and that governs anyway: the worst of the three, because it cannot be audited.

## What the Margin Buys

One detail remains, the one that turns all of this into an author's decision.

A master at −16.2 LUFS sits two and a half decibels below the normalization ceiling of video platforms, which hovers around −14. Normalization, in most cases, lowers the level of what comes in too high; it does not raise what is low. A master below the ceiling is not touched, or is barely touched, and keeps its shape.

That means the margin is not a deficit of energy. It is a purchase. By mastering below the ceiling one pays in volume for what one gains in dynamics: the platform stops intervening in the curve, and the difference between the contained block and the intense block survives playback. It is the only currency left when volume no longer competes, because volume has stopped being a prize.

And here appears the most interesting inversion in the whole affair: when loudness is capped, the only variable still available is where the loudness occurs. The average stays fixed and the distribution stays free. What the system can no longer reward is sustained intensity; what it can still reward is contrast, and contrast is, exactly, the performance.

## The Standard Must Arrive Late

From all this I draw a rule of order, which is the only thing I can defend as method.

The standard must arrive late. The take is recorded without knowing how much it will measure, because a voice recorded for a figure is already mastered before it exists. The choice between two takes is made with the ear, and if the number is going to take part, let it take part as an attached datum and not as a vote. And when the number arrives — because it has to arrive, and does well to arrive — it must arrive with its accounting visible: which gate it used, which stretch it discarded, which part of the performance was left out of the count.

That is what separates a meter from a director. The meter answers a question that someone formulated; the director decides which questions matter. A master at −16.2 LUFS does not say how a voice sounds. It says how much it has to give up in order to enter an ear that was not in the room, through speakers I do not know, in a room whose background noise I cannot measure.

The whole difference between those two sentences — between describing a performance and directing it — fits inside the gate that discards the whisper. And in deciding, each time, whether that gate is a condition of playback or an opinion about how one ought to act.
