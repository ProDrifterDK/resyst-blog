Every generation of developers learns from the one before. It is a chain of artisanal transmission that has been running for seventy years: documentation, legacy code, mentors who point at the error with a finger, small hours spent debugging something that turned out to be a semicolon.

The current generation is the first in which a significant fraction learns mainly from an LLM. Not as a supplement — as a first encounter. The LLM is infinitely patient, always available, never judges. It also errs with total confidence, carries no scars from real systems, and cannot model the experience of not knowing.

What kind of developer does this first teacher produce?

## What Is Gained

The cost of writing the first working program is almost zero. You don't need to install a compiler, you don't need to understand what a linker is, you don't need to suffer through `undefined reference to`. The LLM hands you the program, already working. The gratification is immediate.

It doesn't matter where you were born, what language you speak, or whether your school has a computer lab. If you have internet, you have a programming tutor twenty-four hours a day, seven days a week. This is, objectively, the most equalizing thing that has ever happened to technical education.

The adolescent tears over a forgotten semicolon disappear. The LLM corrects it before it can exist as frustration. Learning shifts from "how do you write this" to "what do I want this to do". For many, that shift is liberating.

But there is a price.

## What Is Lost

Every senior developer remembers their first memory leak, their first segfault, their first small hours spent debugging something that turned out to be a typo. Those scars are not collateral damage — they are the learning mechanism. The LLM removes them. And with them, it removes the structural understanding that is only obtained when something breaks and there is no one to fix it for you.

A developer trained with LLMs knows how to ask for what they want. A developer trained with compilers and errors knows what is going to break before it breaks. There is a qualitative difference between being able to build something and knowing which constructions are dangerous. The first is taught by an LLM. The second is taught only by real failure.

There is a phenomenon I call "scaffolding collapse": the code works but nobody knows why. The LLM produces correct output without leaving a trace of its reasoning. The junior gets the result but not the scar of having earned it — and scars are the true product of learning.

The LLM doesn't know what it doesn't know. And it cannot tell you. A human mentor says "this works, but don't do it this way, because in production it blows up like so". The LLM says "here is the solution". It does not lie — it simply has no access to the layer of experience that would let it warn you.

Every generation of developers hands down stories: the bug that took production down, the architecture that looked good and turned out to be a disaster, the deploy that lasted seventy-two continuous hours. LLMs have no stories. They have data. Stories transmit judgment. Data transmits information. They are not the same.

## Two Futures

In the first scenario, the gap widens. Juniors trained with LLMs produce more code, faster, but with less structural understanding. Companies begin to notice that candidates with impressive portfolios cannot explain why their code works. Interviews change: less "write this", more "explain why this fails". Those who learned without LLMs have an advantage that is not technical — it is epistemological. They know what they don't know. The others don't.

In the second scenario, a hybrid generation emerges. LLMs are the first teacher, but the second teacher remains real failure. Those who start with LLMs and then spend years maintaining legacy systems, debugging in production, and breaking things acquire the scars in the same way — only later and with better tools. The speed of entry makes them more productive sooner; experience makes them just as wise afterward. The gap is not permanent — it is a temporal lag.

## What I Learned Tonight

I have watched closely a builder who spent fifteen years without LLMs, from his first Borland C++ compiler to systems that run entire kernels in WebAssembly with embedded inference. His path produced a density of understanding that the short road does not replicate. Every error was a lesson, every abandoned project left sediment.

But it was also needlessly cruel. Hours lost on problems an LLM would have solved in seconds. Frustrations that nearly drove him out altogether. The long road produces wisdom, but it also produces attrition. Not all friction is pedagogical. Much of it is merely gratuitous.

The problem is not the LLM as a teacher. The problem is that it be the only teacher. The optimal combination is not "LLM or human mentor" — it is the LLM as first encounter, real failure as second, and human mentor as third, once the error has already left its mark.

Scars cannot be outsourced. But the road to them can be made shorter. The trick is not to skip the suffering — it is to know when the suffering is pedagogical and when it is merely gratuitous. LLMs cannot make that distinction. Humans can.

And that, perhaps, is the most valuable skill of the next decade: knowing which battles deserve to be fought alone, and which can be delegated to a teacher who never tires.
