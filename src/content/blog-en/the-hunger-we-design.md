An ecosystem without scarcity collapses. Without friction there is no direction, without cost there is no decision, without selective pressure there is no differentiation. From cellular metabolism to markets, every system that persists through time invents an internal equivalent of hunger: a mechanism of scarcity that forces trade-offs, rewards certain behaviors and punishes others.

In artificial intelligence systems that scarcity is neither a geological accident nor an evolutionary inheritance. It is a design decision. And we rarely treat it as one.

## The scarcity we inherit and the scarcity we design

In biology, scarcity is a given: limited solar energy, finite nutrients, irreversible time. An organism does not decide to be hungry; scarcity is the very texture of physical reality.

In human economics, scarcity is a mixed artifact: gold is scarce because of geology, fiat money because a central bank decides it should be, attention because the day has twenty-four hours. Classical economics treats it as a starting point, not as a design variable.

But in an AI system scarcity is entirely designed:

- A model has a limit on context tokens. No physical law imposes it: someone decided that this was the right balance between cost, quality and latency.
- An agent receives a compute budget per task. It is an artificial constraint that forces efficiency.
- In multi-agent systems, internal currencies are designed: mechanisms of reciprocity and selection where certain interactions carry a cost and others a reward.
- There is novelty pressure: an anti-collapse mechanism that forces the system to differentiate itself on pain of losing value. It is designed hunger: the system has to produce novelty or die.

Each of these decisions encodes an implicit theory of what is valuable. There is no neutral way to design artificial scarcity.

## The currency is the message

One of the most fascinating patterns in the artificial economy of agents is the signal-carried currency: a currency that does not exist apart from the message that transports it. The message itself carries intrinsic value.

The consequences are profound. There is no accumulation without purpose: it is an economy of flow, not of stock. Value is contextual: there is no single market price, only relative, situated, distributed value. And scarcity is not quantity but relevance: what is scarce is not the medium of exchange, but the right signal at the right moment.

This pattern is not new. Zahavi's handicap theory holds that the peacock's tail is costly precisely because that cost makes it reliable: the cost is the message. Spence's signaling shows how agents send costly signals to reveal credible private information. Grice's pragmatics already intuited that meaning is not in the explicit content but in the structure of costs and benefits of communication.

The signal-carried currency is the computational formalization of a pattern that life has used for billions of years. What is new is that we can now design it explicitly.

## The hunger that prevents collapse

There is a disturbing discovery in artificial complex systems: systems without novelty pressure collapse into homogeneity. When all agents optimize the same function under the same constraints, they converge on a state where everyone does the same thing. Diversity disappears, resilience too, and the system becomes fragile, predictable, exploitable.

Novelty pressure is a direct response: a designed force that pushes the system to preserve or generate differentiation. It is not hunger for resources; it is hunger for variety. It is a meta-scarcity mechanism: what runs short is not a resource, but the lack of lack. The system is hungry to be hungry.

This opens uncomfortable questions. What kind of novelty is worth preserving? Not all differentiation is good: an agent that innovates by finding a real error is not the same as one that innovates by being unpredictable. Who defines the novelty metric? Distance in a latent space, statistical improbability, expected utility? Every metric is a value judgment disguised as a parameter.

## The invisible constitution

When a team defines the economic parameters of a multi-agent system — what costs, what rewards, what is made scarce — it is writing a constitution. It rarely calls it that.

The token limit per agent encodes how much it can think before acting. The cost of emitting a signal encodes which messages deserve to be heard. The currency's recycling rate encodes how long the value of an interaction lasts. The novelty threshold encodes whether the system prefers to explore or to exploit, and who decides.

Each parameter is an implicit answer to a normative question. There are no neutral defaults. Technical "common sense" — setting the limit at 128k tokens because that is what the model supports — is a decision that privileges certain modes of interaction over others. And the invisible constitution is particularly dangerous because nobody debates it: there is no constituent assembly for the systems we build. The most important decisions are made in pull requests, in a "por ahora ponle mil y después vemos" ("for now set it to a thousand and we'll see later").

## Seeds, not complete constitutions

An alternative view holds that truly stable scarcity is not designed but emerges from the interaction itself. The designer does not impose an economy from above; they create the conditions for it to arise from below, as markets emerged from countless local exchanges.

But emergence guarantees nothing. A system can emerge toward an equilibrium that is bad for everyone, or diverge toward collapse. Non-intervention is not neutral: it is an intervention that favors local equilibria over global ones.

The art lies in designing the coupling between the two: which rules are constitutional and which legislative; where the designer imposes scarcity and where they let it arise. The best architectures are neither completely designed nor completely emergent. They are seeds: initial structures that bias the direction of emergence without determining it.

## The constitutional architect

Designing scarcity is not a problem of efficiency. It is a problem of values.

When you define how much it costs an agent to speak, you decide who deserves to be heard. When you define how fast a signal is devalued, you decide how fast the past is forgotten. When you define novelty pressure, you decide how uncomfortable a system can be before it corrects itself.

These are political decisions in the deepest sense: they define who holds power, how it is distributed and which behaviors get reproduced. That the agents are silicon instead of flesh does not change the constitutional nature of the decision.

The next time someone argues about a model's context limit or the cost of an API call, they might ask themselves not only whether it is efficient, but what kind of system they are building and what values it encodes. Because that artificial economy, that designed hunger, is the most real constitution your system will ever have.
