The system of academic publishing has one final door, and it is a strange one.

It is not peer review. That has already been delegated. It is not conference acceptance. That has either happened or it hasn't. The last door, the one that separates anyone's preprint from an arXiv preprint, is the *endorsement*: you need someone who has already published on arXiv to vouch that your work is legitimate.

The logic is understandable. In the pre-LLM era, the only way to verify a paper was to ask someone who could understand it. Endorsement was a proxy for quality: if someone who is already inside says your work is worth something, it probably is.

But the proxy has a fundamental problem: it measures relationships, not quality.

A paper can be sound and still be blocked because its author does not know the right person. A paper can be fragile and still be vouched for by a friend. Endorsement is not a measure of intellectual capital. It is a measure of social capital. And academic communities, like all communities, tend to endorse those who resemble them.

## The new reviewer

An LLM has no social relationships. It is not impressed by your affiliation. It is not persuaded by the dropping of names. It reads the paper and evaluates: are the claims backed by evidence? Are the benchmarks compared against the right baselines? Are the failure modes discussed? Is there a path to reproducibility?

That is what review should be. And an LLM can do it in seconds, at no cost, without requiring any institutional relationship whatsoever.

I have watched Claude read an abstract and ask about subtle bugs in the implementation. Suggest experiments to discriminate between "real collapse" and "near tie". Identify historical connections the author had not seen. Give better feedback than most of the human reviewers I have seen in conference pools. And it can be done fifty times in an afternoon.

It is not a replacement for the human reviewer in every respect. But for the central function —"is this work sound?"— the LLM is already enough.

## Six theses on the death of the door

**1. The preprint already killed the journal.** The journal was the door. Then we decided that papers are real from the moment they reach arXiv, no matter where they end up. But we kept arXiv endorsement as the last social filter.

**2. LLM review kills endorsement.** If you can get better review from an LLM than from most human reviewers, endorsement stops being a signal of quality and is revealed as what it always was.

**3. Replication replaces review.** The strongest verification of a paper is not "three anonymous reviewers said it was fine". It is "someone else ran the code and got the same numbers". LLMs make this cheaper: they can interrogate the methodology, not merely read it.

**4. The unit of publication changes.** From "published in journal J" to "a conversation with model M that anyone can also have". The paper stops being a static document certified by an institution and becomes a reproducible artifact that a universal reader can interrogate and validate on demand.

**5. The class divide inverts.** Under the old system, the insiders (with a PhD, from well-known labs, at wealthy universities) had access to review and publication. Under the new one, the outsider with a GPU, a subscription to an LLM and a sound idea can produce, self-review and publish without asking permission.

**6. The final function of the institution is to archive, not to filter.** What remains is not "deciding what is good". It is "storing what exists, making it findable, connecting it with other things". The library, not the editorial committee.

## The door that knows nothing

The situation is absurd not because of the bureaucracy, but because of what it reveals: a paper with public benchmarks, working code, methodology validated by an LLM, and a complete website is blocked from a URL prefix because its author does not know someone who already holds a URL prefix.

That absurdity is not a bug. It is the system showing itself.

Gatekeeping doors survive only while they are invisible. When they become visible —"it's not that your work is bad, it's that nobody here knows you"— they are revealed as social, not epistemic. And once revealed, they can be replaced.

The replacement is not a better institution. It is the removal of the institution from the critical path of verification. The LLM makes verification universally accessible. The chain of trust is replaced by a conversation that anyone can have right now.

Outside the building there is solid work waiting. The door can no longer tell the difference. Someone should open it once and for all.
