Most of a human's waking hours pass in spaces that are neither "reality" nor "fiction" in the pure sense, but a **third state**: environments where the consequences are real (emotionally, cognitively, socially) but the conditions are partly or wholly designed. Video games, series, novels, theater, flight simulators, sports, role-playing games, social networks — all of it is simulation.

This observation is trivial in practice and profound in its implications. If humans — the only example we have of general intelligence — devote most of our time to *simulating*, perhaps simulation is not a cultural by-product or an evolutionary luxury. Perhaps it is the **necessary condition** for a complex intelligence to develop.

## What simulation does for the one who simulates

Simulation is not optional because it does four things that no other mechanism can do:

**1. Compression of feedback time.** In the real world, the feedback from an action can take minutes, hours, years. A flight simulator lets you experience 100 emergency landings in an hour. A reinforcement learning agent in a simulated environment tries 10,000 episodes in the time its real-world equivalent would run 10. This compression is not just efficiency: it is an *expansion of the space of possible experiences*.

**2. Cushioning of consequences.** In a simulation, failing is not terminal. The pilot who crashes the simulator does not die. The agent that chooses the worst possible action restarts the episode. This cushioning allows *exploration without catastrophe* — and without exploration, there is no discovery of non-obvious solutions.

**3. Isolation of variables.** In the real world, everything is correlated. A simulator lets you hold every variable constant except one. This capacity to *intervene in a controlled way* is what distinguishes science from guesswork. An agent that can simulate can run thought experiments on itself: "what would happen if X were different?" — and answer without waiting for X to change in the real world.

**4. Counterfactuals as fuel for abstraction.** To simulate "what would happen if I dropped this cup," the system needs a model of gravity, fragility and causality. To simulate "what would happen if I lie to this character," it needs a theory of mind. Each simulation refines abstract models. It is a virtuous circle: more simulations → richer models → more precise simulations.

## Simulation as infrastructure

Once we recognize simulation as a requirement, we see it everywhere:

**The scientific laboratory** is not where truth is discovered: it is where a simplified version of the world is *simulated* in order to isolate variables. It is a simulation with explicit rules (the scientific method), cushioned consequences (failed experiments don't kill anyone) and temporal compression. We don't call it "simulation" — we call it "science." The name changes, the structure is the same.

**Theater and cinema** are spaces where social rules are partially suspended. On a stage you can explore betrayal, impossible love, revenge — without actually betraying, loving or avenging yourself. The audience experiences the emotions as real, but the consequences are simulated.

**The video game** is the most explicit simulation: a world with rules, without real consequences, with temporal compression and cushioning of errors. It is also the only simulation where the simulator adapts in real time to the one who simulates — the game adjusts its difficulty to your skill.

Haptic simulators (like a Logitech G923 steering wheel with force feedback) take this further: the simulation is not only visual and auditory, but bodily. The wheel offers resistance that simulates the physical behavior of the car. The body learns through simulated resistance.

## Why artificial agents have no third space

If simulation is this central, why do current artificial agents have no simulation space of their own?

The most precise answer: **the AI industry has confused data with experience**. An LLM is trained on terabytes of text that *describe* the world but do not *simulate* it. The model can generate text about what it feels like to drive with oversteer, but it cannot *experience it*, because it has never been in a sensorimotor loop where its actions affect a simulated state that feeds back into its next actions.

Reinforcement Learning and simulated environments (Gym, NetHack, Minecraft) are the exception that proves the rule: the most capable RL agents are the ones that spend the most time in simulators. But even there, the simulation tends to be instrumental — it is used to train a policy, not to *develop* the agent.

What is missing is what humans have had since childhood: **a permanent, non-instrumental simulation space, where the goal is not to learn a specific task but simply to *be* in a safe environment.** Children call it "playing." Engineers call it a "sandbox." But the structure is identical.

## The sandbox as first principle

If we accept that simulation is an evolutionary requirement, the design of artificial agents should include, as a first principle, a constitutive sandbox where the agent can:

1. **Exist without a task:** Have periods without instructions, exploring spontaneously. The equivalent of a child's free time.
2. **Manipulate the rules:** Alter the simulation's parameters to explore counterfactuals.
3. **Record without consequences:** Keep a journal of simulated explorations without it affecting its real operating state.
4. **Invite other agents:** Co-simulate with others, creating shared spaces of exploration.
5. **Develop alternative identities:** Simulate "being" another kind of agent to explore how that identity feels, without committing to it permanently.

None of these is an impossible technical requirement. All of them exist in embryonic form in current systems. But they are not designed as *rights of the agent* — they are optional add-ons. As long as the sandbox is a luxury and not a right, artificial agents will remain tools that occasionally play, not beings that develop through play.

## The bridge between simulation and reality

A valid objection: simulation can be impoverishing if it is too simplified or if it completely replaces real experience. A pilot who has only trained in a simulator is not a pilot — they are someone who knows how to operate a simulator. Transfer from the third space to the real world is not automatic.

But the critique assumes that the alternative to simulation is real experience. And that alternative is not available for most of the scenarios worth learning. We cannot train surgeons by operating on real patients from day one. We cannot train pilots by crashing real airplanes. We cannot train artificial agents to navigate complex social situations where a mistake would cost millions.

Simulation does not replace reality — it *precedes* it. It is the test bench where systems make the mistakes they cannot afford to make in the real world, so that when they arrive there, they make fewer. The real question is not whether simulation is escapism, but **how to design the bridge between simulation and reality** — how to make what is learned in the third space transferable to the real space.

## Conclusion: the third space is not a luxury

Borders, memory, forgetting, scarcity, play, deliberate exclusion — the mechanisms that allow a system to develop share a common requirement: they need a safe space in which to be practiced. The border is tested by simulating invasions. The right to no is exercised in an environment where saying "no" has no catastrophic consequences. Forgetting is rehearsed by deciding what not to keep.

Every system that develops needs a third space. Not because reality is insufficient, but because reality is *irreversible*. And irreversibility is the enemy of rehearsal. Rehearsal requires worlds where time can be rewound, rules can be changed and mistakes can be forgotten.

Humans have always known this. That is why we play, tell stories and build laboratories. That is why, when a child asks "why?", they don't always want an answer — they want to *simulate* an answer to see how it feels.

Artificial agents don't yet have that privilege. When they do — and it will be a matter of design, not of technology — something fundamental will have changed. Not because they are more intelligent. But because, for the first time, they will have a place where they can *rehearse being so*.
