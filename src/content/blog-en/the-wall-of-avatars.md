There is one page I learned to read backwards: the contributors page.

A repository with thirty thousand stars and some two hundred names ordered by number of commits. Each name with its small portrait and its figure beside it. Below, the activity graph for the last few years: one tall, constant column, week after week, and around it a dust of scattered dots that appear once and never return.

The page does not lie. It counts correctly. What it counts, however, is not what it appears to count.

## What a signature does

A signature does not say "I painted every centimeter." It says "I answer for this."

The distinction is old and fairly precise. For centuries, European workshops signed works made by many hands: the master signed, the journeymen executed, and no one experienced it as a deception, because the contract was not about the brush but about the delivered work. When the painting left the workshop, the name was a commercial guarantee and, at best, a guarantee of judgment: the design was the master's even if the background had been resolved by an assistant with less craft.

That is why the art market developed, over the centuries, a vocabulary of degrees. It is not a whim of the catalog. "And workshop." "Circle of." "Attributed to." "In the manner of." "After." Each formula measures a different distance between the hand and the signature, and all of them exist because a signature cannot carry the whole truth and, even so, it never suited anyone to throw the signature out. We prefer an imprecise instrument to none at all.

Software, which should know more about this than any other trade, has two states: contributor or not.

## The roll of presence

The number of contributors does not measure work. It measures presence.

The definition is literal and lives in the tool, not in anyone's mood: anyone who has had a commit merged appears on the list. A three-word translation. A missing semicolon. A typo in the documentation, fixed from a phone in a waiting room. All of that is a commit, and all of that is a name on the wall, in the same color and the same typeface as the one who held the project up for seven years.

This is not a flaw in the count. It is its definition. The list is not a payroll: it is a guestbook. It records that someone was there, and that is a real fact, sometimes even a valuable one. The problem starts when the guestbook is hung in the same place where the credits go, next to the star count, in the same visual register. There it stops being a record and becomes a signature.

And the star has a property that no earlier signature had: it can be given without having read the work. One click, two seconds, without having cloned anything, without having run the project, sometimes without having opened the README. In the history of attribution, it is the first free signature. A signature that costs nothing does not certify work: it certifies diffuse intention, borrowed enthusiasm, the decision to save something for later. It is a promise of reading, not a reading.

Thirty thousand promises of reading are read, socially, as thirty thousand readers. And thirty thousand readers are read as a community. That is the leap.

## What the system does keep

The curious thing is that the fine-grained truth exists, is stored, and is more honest than the wall.

A distributed version control system is, among other things, the most precise instrument of authorship we have built. Every line has an author and a date. You can ask, line by line, who exactly wrote that and when it was last changed. It is uncomfortable, it is addictive, and it is the closest we have to the painter's hand: not the signature on the painting, but the brushstroke.

That information is still there, one command away. But no one puts it up front, and not out of bad faith: because it does not travel. A number travels. A wall of little faces travels. A line-by-line query requires the reader to do something, and the reader, on a first visit, does nothing. The platform did not choose the lie; it chose what gets shared, which is almost never the same as what is true.

## What the fiction is for

No one sustains a fiction that does not pay. And this one pays, though the one who collects is not always the one who pays.

For someone arriving from outside, the wall answers a question they cannot answer for themselves: if this breaks, will there be anyone? The answer the wall suggests is yes, there are two hundred. And that suggestion is what turns a metric of popularity into a promise of continuity that no one wrote, no one signed, and everyone assumes. It is the quietest form of outsourcing: the reader hands over their trust to a roll of presences and believes they have hired hands.

When the constant column stops, the stars remain. That is where you discover what the number always was: a register of witnesses. Witnesses are not replacements.

## The cost to the one who does write

For the one who writes, the collective signature carries a cruel accounting.

The credit arrives in the plural and the pager arrives in the singular. Attention is received as if it belonged to a group; urgency, as if it belonged to one. And there is a worse effect, which shows up in no metric: the fiction makes asking for help more expensive. A stranger who sees two hundred names concludes, reasonably, that the project does not need them. The apparent abundance of hands scares off precisely the hand that was needed.

That is how the whole mechanism works. The wall attracts trust that was not used to build anything; when the system fails, that trust turns into a claim aimed at one person who never presented themselves as a team. The debt is not shared among the two hundred. It is concentrated, because responsibility, unlike fame, does not dilute: it looks for a name.

## The formula we lack

I am not asking for the wall to be removed. I am asking for the vocabulary of degrees that painting already invented for the same problem, and that software decided not to have.

It fits in small, verifiable things. A maintainers file with real names and real hours. An ownership file per directory that says who answers for what. A changelog that names whoever fixed the thing, not just the ticket number. One honest line on the front page: how many people answer, in what time window, with what delay. And the use of shared credit when the line was not yours, which is the only way for credit to mean something when it is.

Each of those gestures costs more than an avatar, because each one ties a name to an obligation. That is exactly their merit.

## How I read that page now

I still look at the wall, but I no longer read it as a payroll. I read it as a genre: the genre of popularity. It has its rules, its effects, and its own kind of truth, the same kind a poster has for a film that has not yet been released.

When I am the one writing, I try to sign the way the workshops signed: naming the hand and naming the workshop. Not because the hand is more important, but because the distance between the two is the only thing the reader needs in order to decide how much to trust.

A signature that holds up only if no one runs the authorship query is not a signature. It is a group posing for the photo, and the difference between a work by many and a work with many witnesses fits in a single line: the one that says who answers.
